\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Protein-coding orthology as a tractable problem}
Orthology inference for protein-coding genes is well established and supported by
scalable annotation transfer pipelines
\citep{kirilenkoIntegratingGeneAnnotation2023,sharmaIncreasedAlignmentSensitivity2017,kentEvolutionsCauldronDuplication2003,sharmaCESAR20Substantially2017,herreroEnsemblComparativeGenomics2016,fitchDistinguishingHomologousAnalogous1970,kooninOrthologsParalogsEvolutionary2005}.
Strong sequence conservation, together with constraints imposed by open reading
frames, enables reliable cross-species mapping of gene loci
\citep{sharmaIncreasedAlignmentSensitivity2017,sharmaCESAR20Substantially2017}.
Methods such as TOGA, which combine genome alignment chains with coding sequence
structure, achieve high accuracy at genome scale. However, these approaches rely
on properties specific to protein-coding genes and do not generalize to
non-coding RNAs. In particular, they assume conserved coding structure and
detectable sequence similarity, which are often absent outside protein-coding
contexts.

In this work, we do not attempt to infer evolutionary orthology. Instead, we
focus on identifying candidate cross-species correspondences supported by genomic
synteny and consistency in embedding space. This formulation is best viewed as
synteny-constrained annotation transfer, where predicted loci represent candidate
corresponding elements rather than definitive orthologs.

\subsection{The ncRNA orthology challenge}
Establishing cross-species correspondence for ncRNAs remains substantially more
difficult. Sequence conservation is often weak or absent across evolutionary
distances
\citep{ulitskyLincRNAsGenomicsEvolution2013,diederichsFourDimensionsNoncoding2014,hezroniPrinciplesLongNoncoding2015,rivasEvolutionaryConservationRNA2021,ulitskyEvolutionRescueUsing2016,statelloGeneRegulationLong2021},
and transcript boundaries are frequently ambiguous, making it unclear what
constitutes a conserved unit
\citep{ulitskyLincRNAsGenomicsEvolution2013,diederichsFourDimensionsNoncoding2014,hezroniPrinciplesLongNoncoding2015,mattickLongNoncodingRNAs2023}.
Functional constraints are not reliably captured by primary sequence alone,
limiting the applicability of sequence-based approaches
\citep{rivasEvolutionaryConservationRNA2021}. In addition, ncRNA annotations are
incomplete and heterogeneous across species
\citep{mattickLongNoncodingRNAs2023,perteaCHESSNewHuman2018}, complicating both
evaluation and transfer. Finally, embedding representations of short ncRNAs can be
context-dependent \citep{chenInterpretableRNAFoundation2022}, making direct
comparison sensitive to boundary definition.

For example, two ncRNA loci may exhibit low primary sequence similarity while
preserving a similar structural pattern:
\begin{verbatim}
GAGGUAUCCAAGAUACCUC
(((((((.....)))))))
AUGCUACGAUCGGUAGCAU
(((((((.....)))))))
\end{verbatim}
In such cases, sequence-based comparison may fail to establish correspondence,
whereas embedding-based representations may capture similarities associated with
shared structural patterns.

\subsection{Limitations of existing approaches}
Existing approaches address complementary aspects of ncRNA detection and
comparison but do not provide a scalable framework for genome-wide ncRNA
correspondence analysis. Covariance models (e.g., Infernal
\citep{nawrockiInfernal11100fold2013}/Rfam \citep{ontiveros-palaciosRfam15RNA2025})
offer high specificity but rely on curated families and do not generalize to
genome-wide discovery. Structure-based alignment methods such as LocARNA
\citep{willLocARNA20Versatile2024} and RNAforester
\citep{hochsmannLocalSimilarityRNA2003} capture structural similarity but are
computationally expensive and not suitable for large-scale analysis. Genome-wide
methods such as RNAz \citep{gruberRNAz20Improved2010}, EvoFold
\citep{pedersenIdentificationClassificationConserved2006}, and FEELnc
\citep{wucherFEELncToolLong2017} identify candidate RNA elements or lncRNA loci,
but focus on detection or annotation rather than establishing cross-species
correspondence. Comparative genome alignments define candidate homologous
intervals but do not provide an RNA-specific measure of similarity or a principled
way to localize relevant signal
\citep{zoonomiaconsortiumComparativeGenomicsMultitool2020,armstrongProgressiveCactusMultiplegenome2020}.
As a result, existing approaches either lack scalability, require strong prior
knowledge, or do not establish explicit correspondence between loci at genome
scale. Together, these limitations motivate a formulation that (i) restricts the
search space via synteny and (ii) defines similarity in a representation space not
tied to primary sequence.

\subsection{Synteny-constrained embedding matching}
A key missing component is the integration of synteny with embedding-based
similarity. Genome alignment chains define a constrained search space of candidate
loci, reducing the combinatorial complexity of cross-species comparison
\citep{zoonomiaconsortiumComparativeGenomicsMultitool2020,armstrongProgressiveCactusMultiplegenome2020}.
At the same time, RNA foundation models such as RNA-FM
\citep{chenInterpretableRNAFoundation2022}, RiNALMo
\citep{penicRiNALMoGeneralpurposeRNA2025}, and related embedding approaches such as
RNABERT \citep{akiyamaInformativeRNABase2022} provide learned representations
associated with RNA family identity, structure-related signal, and downstream
predictive tasks \citep{vahabAdvancingNoncodingRNA2025}. Without synteny
constraints, embedding-based matching would require genome-wide comparison over a
vastly larger search space, making the problem computationally expensive and
increasing the risk of false positives. Combining these sources of information
enables tractable comparison of ncRNA loci in settings where primary-sequence
conservation is limited.

\subsection{Method overview}
We formulate cross-species ncRNA correspondence as matching of
embedding-supported regions constrained by genome alignment chains. Candidate loci
are first identified using a TOGA-inspired projection that leverages alignment
coverage in intronic and flanking sequence to provide locus-level syntenic
context that is partly independent of conservation within the ncRNA itself
\citep{kirilenkoIntegratingGeneAnnotation2023,sharmaIncreasedAlignmentSensitivity2017}.

Within these candidate regions, \curia{} evaluates similarity in embedding space
rather than relying on primary-sequence alignment. The pipeline treats short and
long ncRNAs with complementary similarity measures. For short ncRNAs, each region
is represented as a set of per-position embedding vectors and similarity is
quantified using Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)
\citep{grettonKernelTwoSampleTest2012}, a distribution-level measure over
embeddings; low MMD indicates similar embedding distributions. For long ncRNAs,
each localized subregion is embedded once and matched at nucleotide resolution by
Smith--Waterman alignment \citep{smithIdentificationCommonMolecular1981} over the
per-token cosine similarity dotplot. Both formulations avoid explicit
primary-sequence alignment and provide continuous scores for ranking candidate
matches, making them suitable for ncRNAs where primary-sequence conservation is
weak.

\subsection{Contributions}
In this work, we:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Reformulate cross-species ncRNA correspondence as synteny-constrained
        matching in embedding space.
  \item Introduce \curia{}, a pipeline combining chain-based locus projection with
        embedding-based detection and comparison of localized signal.
  \item Propose an island-based representation for long ncRNAs to localize
        cross-species signal.
  \item Develop a boundary refinement strategy for short ncRNAs.
  \item Demonstrate a genome-scale proof of concept across multiple mammalian
        genomes, showing that this approach can recover cross-species ncRNA
        correspondence in cases where primary-sequence similarity alone is
        limited.
\end{enumerate}
